\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What an uploader can gain.}
On the simulated platforms, the tier choice dominates and is confirmed by every judge. The filter
gain on top of it is small, depends on the content and on the platform, and most of what an
optimiser finds is not confirmed by metrics it did not optimise. What survives is simple: upload at
the higher tier and near-losslessly, and add a mild sharpening only on natural low-motion content.
No tuning is needed for this; FFmpeg's default unsharp already matches a configuration tuned with 195
black-box calls. A learned pre-compensation did not add to it for any content class, even after its
emulator gap was closed. Because whether sharpening helps depends on the platform's rate headroom, the
recipe should be validated on each platform, which costs about 20 uploads.

\paragraph{Why learned pre-processing can look better than it is.}
Two effects compound. A pre-compensation trained through an emulator exploits the emulator's errors:
its predicted gain does not survive the real channel, even on its own training clips, although the
emulator's average pixel error is small. Once this is removed by anchoring the forward value on the
black box, the remaining gain lies in the training loss (LPIPS) and is paid for in metrics the network
never saw. Reported gains of learned pre-processing are therefore informative only when they are
measured on the real channel, against a tuned simple baseline, with metrics outside the training
loss.

\paragraph{Evaluation lessons.}
Three practices changed our conclusions and, we believe, should be standard for pre-processing work:
decomposing the gain into the tier gain and the filter gain; judging with metrics that the optimiser
never sees; and reporting variability across seeds and clips rather than single runs. Without them,
the composite results alone would have read as a consistent filter gain of about three points over
the tier trick on every natural clip.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Most results use simulated platforms whose ladders are plausible but invented; the
        passthrough rule of E12 is invented as well.
        \pending{state what the real-platform probe confirmed or contradicted.}
  \item Clips are 3\,s long and 720p. Natural content comes from professionally shot test
        sequences, not phone-recorded user content.
  \item Class C has a single clip, so the hypothesis about flat and dark content is untested.
        Confirmation splits contain six or seven clips per class, which limits statistical power.
  \item Retiming 24/25\,fps sources to 30\,fps speeds up their motion and raises TI.
  \item The composite objective's weights were set by hand.
  \item The learned pre-compensation is a small residual U-Net trained with one loss family at a
        time; larger networks or losses that include the judges were not tried, since a judge used
        as a loss stops being a judge.
  \item The validation-size estimate (E10) is relative to the 24--27 clips of each class on two
        simulated platforms.
  \item No metric is a substitute for viewers. \pending{viewer study.}
\end{itemize}
